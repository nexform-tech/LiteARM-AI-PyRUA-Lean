# Operating knowledge for RoboDojo with two LiteArm SE arms: how the tools behave (no task recipes)

## Frames, units, robot
- Two LiteArm SE arms (seven joints each, `"left"` and `"right"`) with
  LiteGrip parallel grippers stand side by side at the near edge of a table;
  the head camera looks down the table from the robot's side.  World frame: x
  across the table (the LEFT arm is at negative x and on the LEFT of the head
  image), y away from the robot towards the far edge, z up; metres and
  degrees.  The arms' bases are at x = -0.30 and +0.30, y = -0.45.
- The table top is at z = 0.765.  Read object heights from the scene
  (`locate`), never assume them.
- Each arm starts folded forward over the table: the grasp point at
  `[±0.30, -0.20, 0.95]` with `pitch ≈ 10` and
  `yaw ≈ -10` (the exact joints are the robot's start and the target of
  `home()`).  The wrist cameras see the table at the start.  The seven joints
  are `joint1` .. `joint7`; `joint4` (wrist roll) travels only from -177 to
  +2 degrees and `joint6` / `joint7` (wrist yaw / tool rotation) +-90 degrees.
- The task instruction (`robo.task`) is generated by the benchmark for this
  layout and is authoritative: read it before planning and obey it verbatim.

## The grasp point and the gripper's orientation
- `move_to` drives an arm's GRASP POINT: the middle of the LiteGrip finger
  pads, which is also the point RoboDojo reports as the end-effector pose
  (`state().left.tcp_pos`; `eef_pos` is the same point).  The fingers are long:
  they span 4.6 cm either side of the grasp point along the pointing
  direction, so pointing straight down the grasp point cannot go lower than
  about 4.6 cm above the surface under it.  The fingertips reach 4.6 cm
  BEYOND the grasp point, which is what lets a top grasp reach down to a low
  object: put the grasp point roughly 1 cm above the object's top, not at its
  middle.
- A **hollow object** (bowl, bin) is not a solid top: `top_z` is its rim.  Do
  not aim at the centre — the pads would sit inside the rim on the sloping
  wall.  Aim at the **rim wall**: from the measured centre move outward by
  about `width_m/2 - 1.5 mm` (for the 0.117 m bowls here, 5.7 cm from the
  centre), and put the grasp point at the rim top (`top_z`, never below
  table+4.6 cm).  Close: a finger-face width of 0.3–1.5 cm is the wall; 2–5 cm
  means the pads are on the inner slope — open and re-aim outward/up.  Lift
  3–5 cm and `locate` the bowl; if its centre did not move, the grasp missed.
- The orientation is three angles (`state().left.pitch`, `.yaw`, `.roll`):
  `pitch` 0 points level, 90 straight down and -90 straight up; `yaw` is the
  heading of the pointing direction on the table, 0 away from the robot, +90
  towards the robot's left (-x); `roll` turns the fingers about the pointing
  direction.  The starting pose is folded forward and slightly down
  (`pitch ≈ 10`, `yaw ≈ -10`).
- Pointing straight down (`pitch=90`), `yaw` alone sets how the fingers lie:
  they close along the line at `yaw` degrees from +x (counter-clockwise seen
  from above), and `roll` reads 0.  An EMPTY open gripper's fingers are
  interchangeable, so `yaw` and `yaw + 180` are the same grasp and such a
  move may arrive at either (`note` says so).  Holding something, a move
  always arrives at exactly the angles asked: that is how a held object is
  turned (pouring, standing something up, twisting).
- An angle you leave out of `move_to` keeps its current value: a plain
  position move does not turn the gripper.

## Motion
- `move_to(xyz, arm, gripper, pitch, yaw, roll, path)` solves the arm's
  inverse kinematics for the full pose in the library (not in the simulator)
  and commands joint angles.  With `path="auto"` the grasp point follows a
  straight line, about 2 cm or 8 degrees per native action; where the line
  cannot be followed the joints swing straight to the solution (up to 0.15 rad
  per joint per action) - away from objects only.  `path="line"` is the line
  or nothing: use it for every approach, descent and lift near objects.
- In the simulator, pointing-down targets over the table arrived within
  1 mm and 0.3 degrees, and line descents stayed on the line.  `reached`
  means within 0.01 m and 5 degrees.
- Nothing moves when the pose is out of reach or every way there puts the arm
  into the table: `ik_error` says which, `steps_used` is 0, and for a pose
  out of reach it names nearby poses that ARE reachable ("reachable instead:
  pitch=45 at this position, or z=0.860 with this orientation").  Take one of
  those, or the other arm; do not repeat the pose.  A pose missed by at most
  2 cm and 8 degrees is taken anyway: the arm goes to the closest reachable
  pose and `note` says how far off it is.
- Reach depends on height and pitch (measured from the arm's kinematics and
  joint limits, pointing away from the robot).  The arm's working radius is
  about 0.65 m; a top grasp near the table is limited by the tool-down
  orientation and the long LiteGrip fingers.  With the left arm base at
  y = -0.45, straight down (`pitch=90`) the grasp point reaches to y = 0.13
  (0.58 m in front of the base) at z = 0.85, to 0.11 at z = 0.90, to 0.07 at
  z = 0.95 and to 0.01 at z = 1.0.  A grasp point below z = 0.81 puts the
  4.6 cm fingertips into the table and is refused.  At `pitch=60` the arm
  reaches to y = 0.21 at z 0.90, 0.17 at z 1.0 and 0.11 at z 1.1; at 45 to
  0.27 / 0.23 / 0.17; level (`pitch=0`) to 0.33 / 0.31 / 0.29.  Across,
  pointing down at z = 0.85, the left arm reaches from x = -0.65 to 0.19 at
  y = -0.3, from -0.65 to 0.15 at y = -0.15 and from -0.63 to 0.03 at
  y = 0.0; the right arm is the mirror image.  Beyond those limits the pose
  is refused and `ik_error` names reachable alternatives.
- So: a top grasp suits objects the grasp point can reach (about 0.45 m in
  front of the base, lower than z = 1.0), approached from 6-10 cm above;
  take tall objects (bottles, cups) from the side, level or slightly tilted
  (`pitch` 0-30, `yaw` towards the object), at about half their height.  To
  carry something over a rim higher than about 1.0 m (a box, a holder) hold
  it at `pitch` 30-60 or level: pointing straight down cannot get that high.
  Beyond that reach use the other arm, or push or hand the object closer
  first.  A target the IK misses by at most 2 cm / 8 degrees is taken anyway
  (`note` says so).
- Carrying past things: lift first (straight up, `path="line"`), travel
  above everything in the way, come down over the destination.  A container
  or fixture an arm bumps moves, and most tasks check where it ended up.
  Carry a held block in short legs (about 5-8 cm per `move_to`) and check the
  gripper opening / wrist image after each: a long fast swing can let it
  slide, and the close command then squeezes the empty fingers shut.  Carrying
  moves use a finer joint step automatically (0.025 rad vs 0.15 in free
  space), so a carry costs more native actions than a free move of the same
  length; budget for that.  After
  the close, note the held reading; if the opening falls by more than 0.02
  from it during a carry (about 1.7 mm of width), the block is slipping:
  stop, lower it, and re-grasp - do not continue on a fixed 0.05 threshold.
  If the wide-side grasp slips on a long carry, re-grasp across the narrow
  side (about 1.8 cm): it has more contact area but a smaller target.
- `blocked` set means the arm stopped short of its waypoints: something is in
  the way (an object, the other arm).  Look before trying again.  A descent
  onto a grasp that returns `blocked` may have pushed the object: `reached`
  can still be True (within 1 cm) while the arm stopped more than 5 mm short.
  Do not close after a blocked descent: `locate` the object again; if it
  moved, re-aim, and after a low blocked descent `home` the arm first so the
  new approach starts from the start pose (a different IK branch can leave
  the low descent a few mm short and push the object again).
- The other arm holds its joints during a motion.  Move one arm at a time and
  keep the idle arm out of the working arm's way.
- To twist something held (a key, a cap, a knob), `move_to` the same point
  with another `roll` (straight down: another `yaw`); the last joint turns
  and the grasp point stays.  The LiteArm's tool rotation (`joint7`) stops at
  +-90 degrees, and joint4 (wrist roll) travels only from -177 to +2 degrees:
  a twist is at most a quarter turn per move; turn in steps and check that
  the object followed.
- `home(arm)` returns one arm, or `"both"`, to the joint angles it started
  with and keeps the grippers as they are: a grasp point below 1.0 m first
  goes straight up by up to 6 cm, then the joints swing straight back.  It
  took 15-25 native actions from a reach near the table (17 measured) and
  5-10 when already near the start; test `reached` and call again if it is
  False.  Release what you hold and
  get clear of tall things before it: the swing plans no path.  In a
  multi-block task do NOT `home` between placements: a home right after
  releasing a block on a stack swung the arm through the stack and flung the
  block 23 cm.  Lift straight up 10-15 cm, move sideways clear of the stack,
  and home once at the end (or from a clear pose).
- A press (button, switch, key) is a motion: close the gripper, come down
  pointing straight down over the point, `move_to` a grasp point 1-2 cm
  BELOW where the fingertips would touch (they reach 1.3 cm beyond the grasp
  point) with `path="line"`, then back up.  The fingertips stop where the
  button bottoms out, so `reached` may read False there: measured on
  `swap_blocks`, every command from 1 to 2.8 cm deep stopped about 7 mm below
  the button's top, the bottom of its travel.  Nothing reports whether a press
  registered; look for the effect the task expects.  Count your presses: a
  task that asks for three fails with two or four.
- This station has exactly two arms.  A few benchmark tasks assume a third
  support arm driven by the benchmark (an opponent's moves, a demonstration
  to watch); those tasks are not solvable in this configuration - if the
  instruction refers to a third arm that is not in `state()`, say so and
  finish.  Otherwise, when the instruction says to wait or to watch, home
  both arms and let time pass that way, looking at the head camera.
- For budgeting: in free space a 10 cm line costs about 5-10 native actions,
  but near the table it is much slower - a 10 cm PUSH at z about 0.815 costs
  25-35 (measured 4-5 cm pushes took 24-38), and a 30-40 cm swing at carry
  height about 30-40.  `steps_used` reports each call, and
  `robo.set_gripper` also reports the absolute `step` / `step_limit` (print
  it after any close or open to see where you stand).  Every native action
  counts against the episode's budget (the task card states it: 600 for
  `push_T`, 550 for `stack_blocks`).  Keep your own running total
  (`used += result.steps_used` after every motion) and compare it with the
  card's limit.  When it is spent the state reports `truncated` and no motion
  is possible.  The LiteGrip is the other slow part: a full close from open
  costs about 32 native actions, a full open from closed about 42, a partial
  open 10-26, so budget about 75 per pick for the two strokes alone; a
  cut-off open below 0.8 fails the finish check.  Do not repeat a close that
  missed; re-aim first.

## Gripper
- Two conventions.  Commands are signed: `robo.CLOSE` (1) closes / holds,
  `robo.OPEN` (-1) opens, `robo.KEEP` (0) keeps commanding what the gripper
  was last told (a held object stays squeezed, a partial opening stays).
  Measurements are openings: `state().left.gripper` and `Gripper.opening`
  read 1.0 when open and 0.0 when closed on nothing.
- `set_gripper(arm, command)` runs a full stroke (repeats the command until
  the opening has not changed for three actions) without moving the arms.  At
  the 50 mm/s jaw speed that is about 32 actions for a full close from open
  and about 42 for a full open from closed; a partial open takes 10-26.
  `settled` says the fingers really stopped, and `holding` requires it: a
  stroke cut off by the step limit reports `holding` False even if the opening
  looks large.  After a close, `holding` says the fingers stopped on
  something (wider than about 3 mm across the bare finger faces) and
  `width_m` how wide it is across those faces (`0.0020 m + 0.0850 m x
  opening`: a 3.5 cm block reads 0.035 held across two faces and 0.049
  across its diagonal; within 1 mm on things from 3 to 8 cm).
  The model uses the official finger meshes (no added pad geometry): the soft
  pad is represented by their friction only.  The **finger faces** open to
  8.7 cm and close to 2 mm apart on nothing (the real product closes to 0,
  within its 2.7 mm repeatability).
  `set_gripper(arm, robo.OPEN, opening=0.5)` opens only that far: an object
  `0.0020 + 0.0850 x opening` metres wide then just fits (0.5: 4.45 cm), to
  reach between close neighbours (slices in a rack, tools in a tray) without
  catching them.
- The close is the LiteGrip manual's `close()`: **35 N total** across the two
  fingers (`torque_limit_nm = 3.5`, `force_n x 0.1 N.m/N`); the separate
  `grasp()` hold force defaults to 20 N.  The jaws travel at **50 mm/s** and
  the sim matches that.  The squeeze grows with how far the object keeps the
  fingers from closing, up to 35 N, so a very thin part is held weakly; grasp
  it near its centre of mass and turn it in small steps.
- `holding` is not proof that the INTENDED object is held: confirm that it
  moved with the arm after a lift (wrist camera, or `locate` it at its old
  place and see it gone).  A held object is hard to `locate` because the
  gripper covers it.
- While an arm holds an object, do NOT locate the support with that arm's
  wrist camera: the held object is what it sees.  Use the head camera, or the
  support measurement taken before the pick.
- Carry with `gripper=robo.CLOSE` in every `move_to` that transports an
  object; `robo.OPEN` in a motion drops it.
- Thin and flat parts (handles, boards, a set square) are easy to miss or let
  slip: close across the thin side, and compare `width_m` with the part's
  thickness before lifting; `holding` False or a width far from it is a miss.
- When a thin flat part is a PUSHER rather than the object to move (the white
  set square in align_blocks), do not grasp it: keep the gripper CLOSED,
  lower it to z about 0.80-0.82 behind the square's long side, and push the
  square along the table into the blocks.  The blocks being lined up must
  stay on the table - push them, never lift them.  One locate behind the
  square, one approach, then push; do not spend the 200-action budget
  re-grasping the thin square, and keep about 20 actions for `home("both")`.
- The close width is the object's **thickness along the closing direction**.
  For a flat or rounded object it can be **less** than the footprint's
  `width_m` (a 3.4 cm flat block is about 1.8 cm across its thin side), so do
  not reject a close only because it is narrower than the footprint:
  `holding` plus a lift that carries the object is the test.  A close on an
  edge feels the same, so confirm the lift before carrying it far; if it
  slips, re-aim at the measured centre (a locate taken with the arm over the
  object is biased; use the wrist camera) and close across the other pair of
  faces.
- Verified low grasp on this station's blocks (about 3.3 x 1.8 cm footprint,
  3.2 cm tall): close across the WIDER pair of faces, perpendicular to
  `grasp_yaw` (which is the narrow 1.8 cm side).  From the start/home pose:
  open, `move_to` [x, y, 0.90] with `pitch=90` and that yaw, `path="auto"`;
  descend `path="line"` to z = 0.815 (the grasp point is then about 1.8 cm
  above the block top; below z = 0.812 the fingertips hit the table); close.
  A width of about 3.3 cm is the wide side; lift with `path="line"` to
  z = 0.90.  This carried all three blocks in independent trials (three times
  on the yellow one, once each on the other two), and a 5 cm transport kept
  them.  A close at about 1.8 cm is the thin side: it is a different,
  narrower grasp; verify the lift before carrying.
- For a SMALL object (a few cm), the head camera's centre is a few mm to a
  centimetre off once the arm is over it.  Before the final close, `locate`
  it with the wrist camera of the grasping arm (`cam_left_wrist` /
  `cam_right_wrist`, pointing down at it) and use that centre for x/y; keep
  the head camera's `width_m` as the reference for the close check.
- A top grasp with these tools: `locate` the object; `set_gripper(arm, OPEN)`;
  `move_to` above it pointing down (`pitch=90`, 8-10 cm above `top_z`) and
  `locate` it again from there for the height only (the arm now hides part of
  it, so that centre is biased: keep the first, arm-clear locate's x/y); turn
  to `yaw=grasp_yaw` and
  descend with `path="line"` to about 1 cm ABOVE the object's top (the 4.6 cm
  fingers reach down over it; the grasp point must stay above table + 4.6 cm,
  z = 0.812, or the fingertips hit the table and the pose is refused);
  `set_gripper(arm, CLOSE)`; lift with `path="line"`, and if the line is
  refused as singular or out of reach near the table, repeat the SAME target
  with `path="auto"` (the arm swings through joint space; the refusal names
  it) — at a low table pose that is normal, not a miss.  A low descent can
  also leave the arm's reach: descend to 0.816 or use `path="auto"`.
  For a hollow
  bowl, do not descend to the centre: offset the target to the rim wall as
  above (`width_m/2 - 1.5 mm`) and stop at the rim top; a 0.3–1.5 cm close is
  the wall, 2–5 cm is the inner slope.  A side grasp
  of an upright object: level (`pitch=0, roll=0`: the fingers close
  horizontally), `yaw` pointing from the arm's base at the object, grasp
  point 3-5 cm above its top; then down with `path="line"`, the open fingers
  passing on either side of it, to about half its height.  An approach from
  short of the object, towards the robot, is often out of reach this close to
  the base.
- To put something down, use a support position measured BEFORE the pick or
  from the HEAD camera: while an arm holds a block, its wrist camera sees
  that block, not the support (a held block made a 3.3 cm support read 9 mm
  off and 1.4 cm taller).  A held stack block hangs with its BOTTOM 5.0 cm
  below the grasp point (the grasp point is 1.8 cm above its top; the block
  is 3.2 cm tall).  To place on a support whose top is T, lower with
  `path="line"` until the GRASP POINT is T + 0.050 m plus 2-3 mm, open, and
  lift straight up before moving sideways.  After a block is stacked, the
  next support top is the previous top + the stacked block's height (about
  3.2 cm).  Example: the purple block's top is 0.797 m, so the first block
  goes at grasp z 0.847; once it is stacked, the yellow's top is about
  0.827 m, so the next block goes at 0.877 - not 0.86 (0.86 is only 0.033
  above the stack top and drives the held block into the stack).  If the
  descent returns `blocked` (stopped more than 5 mm short), do NOT open:
  raise 2-3 cm, re-aim at the support centre from the head-camera reading,
  and repeat the descent (`path="auto"` can swing to a pose the line lags);
  only open when the descent reached the place height.
- `place_on(x, y, support_top, tcp_to_bottom)` is the checked placement: it
  descends to `support_top + tcp_to_bottom + clearance`, opens only if the
  descent arrived and was not `blocked` or `slip`, then lifts and retreats.
  Pass the held grasp's own TCP-to-bottom distance (0.050 m for the stack
  blocks; another object or grasp differs).  Do not `home` unless the
  result's `safe_to_home` is true.  After a placement, `locate` the TOP block
  (the one just placed) from the head camera and use ITS centre and `top_z`
  for the next `place_on`: a placed block can end up 1-2 cm off the support's
  original centre (a scripted placement measured 16.7 mm), and the lower
  block is hidden under it.
- A carry `move_to` that reports `slip` has already stopped: the opening fell
  more than 0.02 below the opening the settled close left (the guard keeps
  that close baseline across segments and covers the settling tail and
  KEEP).  The arm does not continue and does not open.  Treat `slip=True` as
  "the block is probably already lost": stop this carry, `locate` the block
  again (head camera on the table, or the wrist camera with an empty
  gripper), and recover explicitly; do not continue the original plan or
  repeat the same segment.  Lower it, open, and re-grasp only as part of that
  explicit recovery.

## Perception
- Three colour cameras, 640x480, and no depth image.  `cam_head` is the
  authority for identity and coarse geometry: which object, which
  destination, the overall layout.  The wrist cameras show what an arm is
  about to grasp or is holding, and a measurement needs one of them to be
  three-dimensional.
- `locate(text, camera)` segments the object and measures it in 3D from the
  cameras that see it: `center_xyz` (footprint centre, half its height),
  `top_z`, `height_m`, the footprint `length_m` x `width_m` (the smallest
  rectangle around it), its long axis `long_axis_deg` (from +x) and
  `grasp_yaw`, the `yaw` that closes pointing-down fingers across its narrow
  side (a cube's: across two faces).  `center_xyz` is the middle of that
  rectangle: a handle, a spout or a tail belongs to the footprint and pulls
  the middle away from the body's.  A hollow one (bowl, bin) gives its rim as
  `top_z`.  Nothing moves.
- One camera cannot tell how tall a thing is: a low deep object and a tall
  shallow one look the same from it.  `views` says how many cameras agreed on
  the measurement.  With `views >= 2` and no `note`, a block, a can, a bowl or
  the body of a bottle is measured to 1-3 mm, its height included.  A `note`
  that the cameras agree only roughly means the second camera saw part of it
  or saw it badly: the position is usable, the height may be centimetres off.
  With `views == 1` the height is a guess, the tallest upright shape that
  fits the one image: close for a box or a can, wrong for a bowl, a cup, a mug
  or anything wider above than below, and for a tall thing that tapers.
  `center_xyz` is then off, along the line away from the robot, by about half
  of what the height is wrong by, and `note` says that it is a guess.
- The view that settles a measurement is from above.  For something low it
  is the one a top grasp passes through anyway: the gripper pointing straight
  down with its grasp point 8-10 cm above the object (`move_to([x, y, top_z +
  0.09], arm, pitch=90)`).  For something tall (a bottle, a holder) or further
  out, where a gripper pointing straight down does not reach, tilt it:
  `pitch=45` to `60`, the grasp point over the object and 5-10 cm above its
  top (for a 19 cm bottle near the robot, `move_to([x, y + 0.04, 1.03], arm,
  pitch=60)`; at that height an arm reaches to about y = 0.05).  The wrist
  camera then looks down on the object while the head camera sees it from the
  side, and `locate` is good to 1-2 mm on a block and 3-4 mm on a bottle, in
  place and in height.  So: `locate` from where you are for a first position,
  move above it, `locate` again, and grasp or place by the second reading.  Do
  the same over the place where something is to be put down (a stack, a slot,
  a container) just before lowering onto it.
- At the start both arms are folded forward over the table: each wrist camera
  sees the table ahead of it, some 40 degrees to either side, and the
  left/right wrist cameras see the left/right bowls.  What lies close to the
  robot's edge of the table, or under a gripper, it does not see.  Measure
  everything you will handle once both arms are out of the way, and check
  `views`.
- An arm over the table hides what lies behind and under it from the head
  camera.  Of an object half hidden, one camera gives a place that can be
  10 cm off; `note` then says that the arm is in front of part of it, or (when
  it was measured taller before) that the arm hides part of it and no
  position is given.  Of an object fully hidden the segmenter returns the
  best OTHER match for your words: a part of the gripper, a different object,
  something beyond the table.  The signs are a `score` below about 0.4, a
  centre far from where the object was, or a `note` that the mask lies on the
  robot's own arm or that what it shows is not on the table (no position
  then either).  In every one of these cases move the arm aside or `home` it
  and `locate` again; never act on a low-score reading that disagrees with an
  earlier good one.
- An object measured once with `views >= 2` keeps its height: a later
  `locate` with the SAME prompt uses it when only one camera sees the object
  then, or when the cameras agree on it less well than they did (`note` says
  so).  A new height replaces it once the old one no longer fits what the
  camera shows (the object fell over).
- `locate` takes the object to stand on the table.  Something lying on top of
  another object, inside a container or in a gripper is not measured
  correctly (it comes out further away and larger): work out where it is from
  where you put it and from the images.
- `segment` + `back_project` give one mask and one point.
  `back_project(row, col, camera)` segments whatever is under that pixel,
  measures it as `locate` does and returns where the pixel's ray meets it
  (`surface == "object"`); a pixel on the table top itself returns that point
  of the table, exactly (`surface == "table"`), and so does one on something
  too flat or too far to measure.  A thin part in mid-air (a peg, a handle)
  is not measured reliably.
- A flat pad or mat lying on the table (the gray T-shaped pad in `push_T`) is
  too flat for `locate`: it comes back `found: false`, or `surface: "table"`.
  Do not push toward a guessed place.  Read TWO pixels on the pad in
  `cam_head` with `back_project` - a pad pixel returns the exact table point
  it covers - take their midpoint as the pad centre and the line between
  points on its stem as its heading.  `locate` and `back_project` are
  read-only: they spend no native actions, only a tool call, so re-read after
  every push.  Push the block until its stem points the same way: the block
  slides further than the arm moves.
- All of them read the CURRENT observation: localise, act, then localise
  again.  Positions from before a contact are stale.
- A mask is the best match for the text, not proof of identity.  Describe one
  object by colour, shape and position; check `score` and where `centroid`
  falls in the image before acting on it.  If a name finds nothing, try what
  it looks like (`"yellow cube"` for a textured block).  Words like "left"
  or "front" do not make it choose another of several alike objects: when two
  prompts give the same centre they found the same object; tell instances
  apart in the image (or with `segment`'s box) instead.  What the robot's own
  arm hides of an object is not measured: it comes out smaller and
  off-centre (move the arm away first).
- `robo.image(camera)` returns the recorded picture as an array for your own
  analysis.

## Judging progress
- Nothing reports task success while the episode runs: no result carries a
  task-success flag.  Judge progress from the images and the robot state,
  and say so when you cannot tell.
- Grasp: the target leaves its place and moves with the arm.
- An amount the instruction names ("lift it by 10 cm") is measured on the
  object, not on the gripper: a held object sags and slips a little in the
  fingers.  Go 2-3 cm beyond the amount asked.
- Transport: the hold stays stable through the lift and the lateral move.
- Placement: the object rests where the instruction wants it after the
  gripper has opened and the arm has withdrawn; an object on a rim or beside
  its destination is not placed.
- The benchmark checks the final scene strictly: positions to about a
  centimetre and angles to a few degrees of the arrangement asked (a row is
  straight, a stack centred, a part upright); EVERY object the instruction
  names; containers and fixtures where they were and upright; the exact
  number of presses; in a task that asks to push or slide something, lifting
  it can fail the task.  "Nearly" is a failure.  Before finishing, `locate`
  each object again, compare with what is asked, and correct what is off
  while budget remains.
- In a pushing task (`push_T`) the block must end ON the pad, centred and
  pointing the same way, not merely beside it, and it must not be lifted.
  `locate` the red T after EVERY push (read-only: no native actions) and
  compare its centre and heading with the pad's two `back_project` points;
  if the heading is off, push one END of the crossbar a short way to rotate
  it - pushing the middle only translates.  Open the gripper only when the
  measured offset is within about 5 mm and the heading within about 5
  degrees.  Stop pushing once your running `steps_used` total passes 500 of
  600: open the gripper (a full open takes about 42 actions), `home` the arm
  you used (15-25), then call `finish(status="success")`.  A cut-off open
  below 0.8 and an arm left away from its start both fail the finish check;
  opening the gripper is not the end.
- What else is checked: objects you did not mean to move (things you knock
  over or out of place count against you: reach between close neighbours
  with a narrower opening); plurals ("the wooden blocks" means all of them,
  whatever their colour); articulated parts pushed to the end of their travel
  (a lever most of the way down, a lid shut within a few degrees; a free lid
  swings when touched); liquids: nearly all of it in the target at the moment
  the container comes back upright, and the container upright at the end -
  liquid visible in a cup is not enough.
- Most tasks count as done only once BOTH arms are back at the pose they
  started in (within 0.15 m and 20 degrees) with BOTH grippers open (an
  opening above 0.8, for a few tasks 0.9), the scene being as the instruction
  wants it at that moment - whether or not the instruction says to reset the
  arms.  So release what you hold, `set_gripper(arm, robo.OPEN)` on both arms,
  lift clear and end with `home("both")`.  Keep about 20 native actions for
  it; nothing is lost where a task does not ask for it.
- Finish when the scene shows that the instruction is satisfied and the arms
  are back and open.

## Safety and recovery
- Inspect the scene after motion.  Stop the current plan if an object is
  unstable or an arm is obstructed, and re-localise before recovering.
- An object that has left the table may be unrecoverable.
- After a failed phase name what went wrong (wrong object, missed grasp, lost
  hold, unreachable target, premature release) and change one meaningful
  variable; do not repeat the same target or the same recovery twice.
- There is exactly one episode and no reset.  Recover in place: re-observe,
  re-localise, re-grasp.
